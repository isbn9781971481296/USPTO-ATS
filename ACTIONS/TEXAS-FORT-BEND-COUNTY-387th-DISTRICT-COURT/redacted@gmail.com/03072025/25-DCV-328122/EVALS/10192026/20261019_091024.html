⚠️ Adjudicator Liabilities & Warnings:

Notwithstanding Candidate #1's relative superiority within the candidate pool, the panel unanimously concludes that it remains highly unlikely to succeed in obtaining an order from Judge Kenneth M. Hoyt quashing the state court hearing notice or enjoining the state proceedings, due to four insurmountable federal jurisdictional and statutory bars: (1) Judicial Officer Immunity under 42 U.S.C. § 1983: Following the Federal Courts Improvement Act of 1996 (FCIA), § 1983 explicitly dictates that 'in any action brought against a judicial officer for an act or omission taken in such officer's judicial capacity, injunctive relief shall not be granted unless a declaratory decree was violated or declaratory relief was unavailable.' Candidate #1 seeks an immediate preliminary injunction restraining Defendant Judge Oscar Telfair III from conducting hearings, which is statutorily barred in the Fifth Circuit (see Bauer v. Texas, 341 F.3d 352, 357 (5th Cir. 2003)); (2) Younger Abstention & Moore v. Sims: Under binding Fifth Circuit and Supreme Court precedent, federal district courts must abstain from interfering in pending state domestic relations proceedings that implicate paramount state interests in family welfare and the enforcement of state court contempt orders, absent a showing of bad faith so egregious that no state corrective process exists (Moore v. Sims, 442 U.S. 415 (1979); Wightman-Cervantes v. Texas, 2005 WL 774843 (5th Cir. 2005)). Although Plaintiff argues that state habeas petitions were summarily denied, the Fifth Circuit treats summary appellate denials as exhausted state avenues rather than proof of systemic bad faith or futility; (3) Anti-Injunction Act (28 U.S.C. § 2283): While Mitchum v. Foster recognizes § 1983 as an expressly authorized exception, federal courts routinely decline to invoke this exception to halt state court civil contempt hearings where state appellate mandamus or final appeal remains the proper corrective vehicle; and (4) Procedural Anomaly of 'Quashing' a State Notice: A United States District Court does not sit as a direct appellate overseer or calendar administrator of a Texas district court, and lacks the procedural authority under the FRCP to 'quash' a state court's internal notice of hearing. The appropriate federal procedural vehicle is a Motion for Preliminary Injunction or Temporary Restraining Order under FRCP 65 directed strictly against non-judicial parties, rather than a motion to quash state notices.
